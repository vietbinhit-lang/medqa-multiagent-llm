Severe hyperkalemia, especially with ECG changes such as peaked T waves and a widened QRS complex, is a medical emergency because of the risk of life-threatening arrhythmias. The first priority is cardiac membrane stabilization, achieved with intravenous calcium gluconate (or calcium chloride), which does not lower the serum potassium level but rapidly protects the heart from the effects of hyperkalemia by stabilizing the cardiac membrane. After membrane stabilization, treatments that shift potassium intracellularly are given, such as intravenous insulin with concurrent glucose (to prevent hypoglycemia) and inhaled beta-agonists. Potassium-removal strategies, such as oral sodium polystyrene sulfonate, loop diuretics, or hemodialysis for refractory or severe cases (especially with renal failure), are used afterward or in parallel, but they act more slowly and do not address the immediate cardiac risk the way calcium does.
