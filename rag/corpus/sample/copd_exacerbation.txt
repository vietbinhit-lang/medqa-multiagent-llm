An acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) presents with worsening dyspnea, increased cough, and increased sputum production or purulence, often with wheezing and tachypnea. Initial therapy centers on inhaled short-acting bronchodilators (a short-acting beta-agonist such as albuterol, often combined with a short-acting anticholinergic such as ipratropium) to relieve airflow obstruction, together with a short course of systemic corticosteroids to reduce airway inflammation and shorten recovery time. Antibiotics are added when there are signs of a bacterial trigger, such as increased sputum purulence. Controller therapies like inhaled corticosteroids alone, without bronchodilators, are not appropriate as acute rescue treatment. Supplemental oxygen is titrated carefully in COPD patients to avoid worsening hypercapnia, and intubation is reserved for impending respiratory failure rather than used as routine initial therapy.
