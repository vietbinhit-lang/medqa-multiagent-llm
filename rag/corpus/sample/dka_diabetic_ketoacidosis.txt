Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) typically occurs in patients with type 1 diabetes (though it can occur in type 2) and presents with hyperglycemia, an anion-gap metabolic acidosis, and ketosis. Clinical features include polyuria, polydipsia, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, deep rapid (Kussmaul) breathing, a fruity odor on the breath from acetone, and altered mental status in severe cases. The cornerstone of initial management is aggressive isotonic intravenous fluid resuscitation (typically normal saline) to correct volume depletion, started before insulin therapy. Potassium levels must be checked and, if low or low-normal, potassium repletion should begin before or alongside insulin, because insulin drives potassium intracellularly and can precipitate dangerous hypokalemia and arrhythmias. Only after fluids are underway (and potassium addressed) is intravenous insulin therapy started, usually as a continuous low-dose infusion, to correct the acidosis and hyperglycemia gradually. Bicarbonate is not routinely given except in severe, life-threatening acidosis.
