Acute pancreatitis presents with severe epigastric pain that often radiates to the back, accompanied by nausea and vomiting, and is supported by a markedly elevated serum lipase (generally considered more specific than amylase). Common causes include gallstones and heavy alcohol use. The mainstay of initial management is aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation to address third-spacing of fluids and prevent hypoperfusion, along with adequate pain control and bowel rest initially, with a gradual, cautious reintroduction of oral intake as tolerated rather than rapid advancement to a regular diet. Broad-spectrum antibiotics are not given routinely to all patients with acute pancreatitis, since most cases are sterile inflammation; antibiotics are reserved for documented infected pancreatic necrosis or another confirmed infection. Immediate surgical debridement is not first-line management and is reserved for specific complications such as infected necrosis that fails less invasive management.
