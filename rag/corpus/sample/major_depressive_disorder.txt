Major depressive disorder is diagnosed when a patient has at least two weeks of depressed mood or anhedonia, along with associated symptoms such as sleep disturbance, poor concentration, appetite changes, or feelings of worthlessness, causing significant functional impairment. For patients without contraindications, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are considered first-line pharmacologic treatment because of their favorable side-effect and safety profile compared with older agents. Monoamine oxidase inhibitors and tricyclic antidepressants are effective but are generally reserved for treatment-resistant cases due to more difficult side-effect profiles and dietary or cardiac safety concerns. Benzodiazepines may help with associated anxiety but do not treat the underlying depressive disorder and carry dependence risk. Antipsychotics are not first-line monotherapy for uncomplicated major depressive disorder without psychotic features.
