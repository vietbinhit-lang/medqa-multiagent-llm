Primary hypothyroidism results from failure of the thyroid gland itself (most commonly due to autoimmune Hashimoto thyroiditis in iodine-sufficient regions) to produce adequate thyroid hormone. Typical symptoms include fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin, and sometimes bradycardia. The characteristic laboratory pattern is an elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), reflecting the pituitary's attempt to stimulate an underactive thyroid, together with a low free T4. This pattern is the opposite of Graves disease (an autoimmune cause of hyperthyroidism with low TSH and high thyroid hormone levels) and differs from subacute thyroiditis, which can transiently present with a hyperthyroid phase (low TSH, elevated thyroid hormones due to release of preformed hormone) before potentially evolving into a hypothyroid phase. Secondary hyperthyroidism, caused by a TSH-secreting pituitary process, is rare and would show an elevated TSH with elevated, not low, thyroid hormones.
